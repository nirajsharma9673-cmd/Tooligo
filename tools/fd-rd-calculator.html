How It Works — Fixed Deposit

In a Fixed Deposit, you invest a lump sum for a fixed period at a predetermined interest rate. The interest is compounded at regular intervals (quarterly, monthly, etc.). The formula used is:

A = P × (1 + r/n)n×t

Where P = principal, r = annual rate (as decimal), n = number of compounding periods per year, t = tenure in years. More frequent compounding yields slightly higher returns.

How It Works — Recurring Deposit

In a Recurring Deposit, you invest a fixed amount every month for a set period. Since each monthly deposit earns interest for a different length of time, the standard RD formula (used by Indian banks with quarterly compounding) is more complex:

M = R × [ ((1+i)n − 1) / (1 − (1+i)−1/3) ]

Where R = monthly deposit, i = (annual rate / 400), n = number of quarters (tenure in months / 3). This formula accounts for the fact that each deposit is made at a different point in time and earns interest for a different duration.

Worked Example

FD Example: Rs 1,00,000 at 7.5% p.a. for 3 years, compounded quarterly. A = 1,00,000 × (1 + 0.075/4)4×3 = 1,00,000 × 1.2502 = Rs 1,25,025. Interest earned: Rs 25,025.

RD Example: Rs 5,000/month at 7.5% p.a. for 5 years (60 months). Quarterly rate = 7.5/400 = 0.01875. Number of quarters = 60/3 = 20. M = 5,000 × [(1.0187520 − 1) / (1 − 1.01875−1/3)] = Rs 3,66,333 (approx). Total deposited: Rs 3,00,000. Interest earned: Rs 66,333.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the RD formula look different from the FD formula?

The FD formula calculates compound interest on a single lump sum deposited at the start. In an RD, each monthly deposit is made at a different time, so each earns interest for a different number of months. The RD formula aggregates all these into a single calculation — it’s mathematically equivalent to calculating the future value of each monthly deposit separately and summing them, but much more compact.

Does compounding frequency matter?

Yes. More frequent compounding (e.g., monthly vs. annually) results in slightly higher returns because interest is calculated on a growing principal more often. For a Rs 1,00,000 FD at 7.5% for 3 years: quarterly compounding gives Rs 1,25,025, while annual compounding gives Rs 1,24,230 — a difference of about Rs 795. Indian banks generally compound FD interest quarterly for most tenures.

Is this exact to what my bank will pay?

This calculator provides a very close estimate based on the standard formulas used by Indian banks. However, individual banks may apply slightly different rounding rules, offer special rates for senior citizens or specific tenures, or use minor variations in the RD formula. Always confirm the exact maturity amount with your bank before making investment decisions.

Is FD interest taxable?

Yes. Interest earned on FDs and RDs is treated as “Income from Other Sources” and is taxable as per your applicable income tax slab. Banks deduct TDS (Tax Deducted at Source) at 10% if the total interest exceeds Rs 40,000 in a financial year (Rs 50,000 for senior citizens). You can also check your overall tax liability using our Income Tax Calculator.

What are typical FD and RD tenures in India?

FDs typically range from 7 days to 10 years, with most common tenures being 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years. RDs typically run for 6 months to 10 years, with 5 years being a common choice. Senior citizens often get 0.25% to 0.75% higher interest rates on FDs. Minimum deposit amounts vary by bank — RD minimums are often as low as Rs 100–Rs 1,000 per month.
